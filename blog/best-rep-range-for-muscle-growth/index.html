Best Rep Range for Muscle Growth:
5, 10, 15, or 30 Reps?

Stop chasing a magic number. Choose the right reps for the exercise, train with sufficient effort, and progress over time.

AdonisIQ EditorialOctober 31, 20269–11 min read

Key takeaways: Hypertrophy is possible across a broad range of repetitions. Heavy loads have a strength advantage; moderate and higher reps can be excellent for muscle growth. Pick rep targets by exercise, control effort with RIR, and progress when your technique and performance justify it.

The short answer: there is no magic 8–12 rep window

Muscle can grow across a broad spectrum of repetition ranges when sets are sufficiently challenging. The traditional 8–12 range is useful, but it is not the only effective choice. Heavier sets are generally more efficient for maximal strength, while moderate and higher repetitions can provide excellent hypertrophy stimuli. Very light sets often require more repetitions and more effort to become effective.

What the research actually says

The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine position stand supports resistance training across a range of loads for muscle hypertrophy and identifies heavier loads as particularly useful for strength. Meta-analyses comparing low- and high-load resistance training generally report similar hypertrophy when sets are performed with adequate effort, while strength gains favor heavier loading. These findings do not imply that every rep range is equally comfortable, time-efficient or appropriate for every exercise.

Which rep range should you choose?

3–5 reps: useful primarily for strength-oriented compound work, but heavier loads demand more preparation and careful technique. 6–10 reps: a practical blend of strength and hypertrophy for presses, rows and squats. 10–15 reps: versatile for both compounds and isolation exercises. 15–20 reps: often comfortable for lateral raises, curls, leg extensions and other isolation work. 20–30 reps: can build muscle with lighter loads, though the sets can be uncomfortable and cardiovascular fatigue may become limiting. These are programming heuristics, not hard physiological boundaries.

How close to failure should you train?

Repetitions in reserve (RIR) estimate how many more clean reps you could have completed. A practical default for many working sets is approximately 1–3 RIR. You do not need to reach absolute failure every time; going to failure may add fatigue, especially on heavy compound movements. On a safer machine or isolation movement, an occasional near-failure set can be easier to manage. RIR is an estimate, so refine it with experience.

Real example: incline press versus lateral raises

For an incline press, prescribe 3 working sets of 6–10 reps at about 2 RIR. Suppose you complete 10, 10 and 9 clean reps. Keep the load next time and aim to improve the final set. Once you consistently reach the top of the range at the prescribed effort and with stable technique, increase the load by the smallest practical increment. For lateral raises, use 3 sets of 12–20 reps at roughly 1–2 RIR. Progress reps first, then raise the weight when you own the upper end without swinging. These are illustrative coaching examples, not automatic guarantees of how Adonis Forge will change a program.

When should you increase weight?

A simple double-progression rule is to hold the load steady until you reach the top of your prescribed rep range across your target working sets at the intended RIR. Then increase weight modestly and rebuild repetitions. If a load jump causes technique to deteriorate or sends reps far below the target, use a smaller jump or stay at the prior load. Progression can also mean cleaner reps, better control or more work at the same effort—not only more plates.

Rep range does not replace weekly training volume

A challenging set of 8 and a challenging set of 15 can both contribute to muscle growth, but neither tells you how many weekly sets you need. Track total productive work, recovery and performance over time. For more detail, see our guide to weekly sets per muscle. Training frequency and exercise selection help you distribute that volume without sacrificing execution.

Frequently asked questions

Are 5 reps enough to build muscle? Yes, sufficiently challenging low-rep sets can stimulate hypertrophy, though they are often better suited to heavy compound lifts. Are 20–30 reps too many? No; they can work for hypertrophy, but sets may become uncomfortable and take longer. Is 8–12 best? It is convenient, not uniquely superior. Should every set go to failure? No; close-to-failure work can be effective without routinely reaching absolute failure. Should beginners use high reps? Beginners can learn technique with moderate loads and manageable rep ranges before pushing very heavy or exhausting sets.
