Body Recomposition Workout Plan:
3, 4, or 5 Days

One training framework, three practical schedules. Learn how to select exercises, progress your lifts, place cardio and adjust based on recovery.

By AdonisIQ EditorialOctober 14, 202613–16 min read

A body recomposition workout plan should be built around your life—not the other way around. Whether you lift three, four or five days a week, you can organize productive resistance training around the same core movement patterns, then adjust volume and nutrition using your recorded results. This guide includes complete sample schedules, set and rep targets, double progression, cardio placement and practical recovery decisions.

Key takeaways

  • Body recomposition is real. Resistance training, adequate protein and appropriate energy intake can support muscle gain and fat loss together. Novices and returning lifters may see faster progress, but trained lifters can recomp too.
  • Choose 3, 4 or 5 days. All three plans cover squat, hinge, push and pull patterns. More days mainly let you distribute training across shorter sessions.
  • Weekly volume matters. Start with recoverable sets rather than treating 10–16 weekly sets per muscle as a universal minimum.
  • Use double progression. Add high-quality repetitions first, then increase load after reaching the top of your range.
  • Use trends, not one-day readings. Compare training performance, waist, weight averages, sleep and subjective fatigue before changing your plan.

Is body recomposition real—and who benefits?

Yes. Body recomposition means gaining muscle while losing fat over the same period. A peer-reviewed review by Barakat and colleagues describes recomposition even in some resistance-trained populations. Results depend on training quality, experience, starting body composition, diet and recovery.

Beginners, returning lifters and people with more fat to lose often have more room to improve. But body fat thresholds such as 15% for men or 25% for women are not established eligibility cutoffs. Already-lean experienced lifters can see slower gains, and there is no guarantee of simultaneous progress for any group.

Do not judge recomp by scale weight alone. Track consistent waist measurements, comparable progress photos, average weight trends and several repeatable lifts. Apple Health can provide useful supported activity, sleep and body-measurement trends; the details of training loads and exercises still depend on what you log.

The master template: movement patterns, sets and recovery

Organize each week around a squat or knee-dominant movement, a hinge or hip-dominant movement, horizontal pushing, horizontal and vertical pulling, and supplemental shoulders, arms, carries or core work. On three days, combine patterns in full-body sessions. On four or five days, spread them across sessions. You do not have to squat, hinge and press hard every day.

For demanding lifts, warm up with light practice sets before counting working sets. Many compounds fit well in a 5–10 rep range; accessories commonly use 8–15, with some isolation work at 12–20. Hypertrophy can occur across a wider rep range when effort is adequate. Start with roughly two or three reps in reserve on most working sets, and refine as you gain experience.

How many hard sets per muscle each week?

The 2026 ACSM resistance-training position stand describes roughly ten weekly sets per muscle as a useful hypertrophy reference point. It is not a mandatory minimum. A beginner may progress on fewer; more advanced lifters may gradually build toward 10–16 or more for selected muscles if performance and recovery justify it.

3-day body recomposition workout: full body

Best for: beginners, busy schedules and lifters who want a recovery day between sessions. Use Monday, Wednesday and Friday—or any three nonconsecutive days. You can swap equivalent exercises for equipment availability or comfort.

Rest roughly two to three minutes for demanding compounds and as needed for accessory work. With a warm-up, sessions may run 50–75 minutes depending on setup and rest periods. Because training happens three times per week, each individual session can use modest set counts while the whole week still provides meaningful work.

Important: a three-day plan isn't a weaker version of five days. When productive volume is comparable, consistency and progressive overload matter much more than the number of calendar days.

4-day body recomposition workout: upper / lower

Best for: intermediate lifters or anyone who prefers more exercise variety with less crowded sessions. A classic Monday/Tuesday/Thursday/Friday schedule alternates upper and lower days, allowing major muscle groups two direct sessions each week.

Put the most important lift early, when attention and energy are highest. If time runs short, remove a lower-priority accessory rather than rushing your compound work. Machine and dumbbell exercises are valid substitutions; barbells are not required for effective progressive resistance training.

5-day body recomposition workout: push / pull / legs / upper / lower

Best for: lifters who can reliably attend five sessions and recover well from them. This split uses three focused days followed by an upper/lower repeat, so most major muscle groups receive work more than once weekly. Suggested rest or easy-activity days are Thursday and Sunday.

The five-day version can make individual sessions shorter, but it is not automatically superior. If you consistently miss the fifth workout, lifts regress or fatigue accumulates, a four-day split may be the more productive choice.

What changes between the three schedules?

Distribution changes; fundamentals don't. All versions prioritize repeated movement patterns, quality sets, and logged progression. Training frequency helps divide weekly volume across workable sessions. Research comparing frequencies suggests that when weekly volume is matched, higher frequency is not necessarily more hypertrophic. Technique practice and preference still matter.

Choose a plan you can follow for several months. Three well-executed days beat a five-day schedule that repeatedly falls apart. See our first 12 weeks of recomposition for a beginner-friendly overview of the full process.

Rep ranges and double progression

For an exercise prescribed as 3 × 6–10, start with a load you can control for at least six good repetitions in each set. Over the following sessions, add repetitions as technique and effort allow. Once you reach ten reps on all three sets, raise the load slightly and return toward the bottom of the rep range.

Example: 100 lb for 8, 8, 8 progresses to 9, 8, 8; eventually 10, 10, 10; then perhaps 105 lb for 8, 8, 8. Increasing reps, load or movement quality with comparable effort counts as progress. No single session has to set a record.

What should you do if progression stalls?

Two stagnant sessions are information, not a mandate to deload. Check sleep, diet, technique, rest times, soreness, stress and whether other training has grown. If several lifts are regressing and fatigue accumulates, consider an easier week with around 30–50% fewer hard sets and loads adjusted as needed. Resume progressively, rather than trying to recover missed volume all at once.

Wearable sleep trends and resting heart rate can help you notice changes, but three poor readings are not validated automatic deload triggers. Personal symptoms, perceived effort and actual lifting performance remain essential context.

Where does cardio fit without compromising muscle?

Walking, incline treadmill sessions, cycling, rowing and other aerobic activities can support cardiovascular fitness and total energy expenditure during recomp. Begin with an amount you can comfortably recover from—such as two or three 20–30 minute conversational-pace sessions—and build from there based on your fitness and health goals.

The literature is more nuanced than “cardio kills gains.” The 2022 updated concurrent-training meta-analysis found that combining aerobic and strength exercise did not meaningfully compromise overall muscle hypertrophy or maximal strength on average, although explosive-strength adaptation appeared more sensitive to same-session combinations. Some fiber-level findings suggest the type and amount of running may matter.

Put cardio after lifting or on other days when convenient. If combining hard endurance and strength sessions, separate them by several hours when practical, especially if explosive performance matters. Watch your leg-session quality and recovery; adjust difficult cardio before sacrificing productive lifting volume. You do not need a universal six-hour gap for easy walks.

Calories, protein, carbohydrates and training days

Recomp can happen around estimated maintenance calories or in a modest deficit when fat loss is a priority. 200–400 kcal below estimated maintenance is a reasonable starting option for some people, not a rule for everyone. Larger deficits can make recovery and strength progression harder. Compare intake with weight and waist trends over two or three weeks before adjusting.

A protein intake of approximately 0.7–1.0 g per pound of bodyweight per day (around 1.6–2.2 g/kg) is a practical target for many healthy resistance-training adults. The ISSN protein position stand identifies 1.4–2.0 g/kg for most exercising adults, with context-specific higher intakes during dieting. People with medical restrictions should follow individualized professional guidance.

Worked example: 180-pound lifter

Suppose estimated maintenance is 2,600 calories and a small deficit suggests about 2,300 calories a day. One illustrative daily split is 180 g protein, 230 g carbohydrate and 73 g fat (approximately 2,300 calories). Your optimal intake may differ substantially; these aren't targets to copy automatically.

Carbohydrate supports training, but you don't need complicated cycling. You can move more carbs toward a hard squat or hinge day and eat less on a rest day if you prefer, while accounting for total weekly energy. Adding 50–100 g of carbohydrate adds about 200–400 calories unless other intake is reduced. Keep daily protein consistently adequate.

What results should you expect at 8, 12 and 16 weeks?

Recomp is often gradual. No credible plan can promise exactly half an inch off your waist at eight weeks or a 10% strength gain by week sixteen. Use these as review checkpoints rather than guaranteed transformations.

8 weeks: confirm the plan is taking hold

Compare your exercise technique, loads and reps with your baseline. Check waist and weight averages alongside consistent photos. Early strength improvements may reflect neural and skill adaptations rather than visible muscle growth.

12 weeks: look for meaningful trends

Ask whether your waist, photos, training performance and adherence are moving toward your goals. A relatively stable scale with a smaller waist and improved lifts may be consistent with progress, but no single metric proves the exact changes in fat and muscle.

16 weeks: choose your next phase

If training is progressing and your measurements are changing as desired, keep going. If multiple lifts have stalled and fatigue is mounting, reassess food intake, volume and recovery. If your main priority has shifted toward muscle gain or faster fat loss, consider a deliberate adjustment rather than continuing by habit.

Frequently asked questions

Is body recomposition real or marketing?

It is real. Evidence documents simultaneous muscle gain and fat loss, including in some trained people. Your results depend on training, nutrition, baseline body composition and recovery.

Can women follow this plan?

Yes. The same movement patterns and programming principles apply; select exercises, sets and nutrition based on your individual ability, recovery and goals. There is no validated sex-specific body-fat threshold that determines whether recomp works.

Can three days of training be enough?

Yes. Three full-body sessions can provide ample productive resistance training, especially for beginners. A plan you can consistently complete usually beats a theoretically optimal schedule you cannot maintain.

What macros should I eat?

Many resistance-training adults start with about 0.7–1.0 g protein per pound daily, at maintenance calories or in a small deficit when fat loss is a priority. Adjust carbohydrates and fats to preferences, training demands and dietary needs.

How quickly will I see results?

Strength and technique may improve within weeks. Visible body-composition changes often require longer. Review waist measurements, weight averages, photos and lifting progress after 8, 12 and 16 weeks without expecting fixed numerical gains.
