How Many Sets Per Muscle Per Week
Do You Really Need to Grow?

More sets can build more muscle. But more isn't always better. Here's how to find your productive weekly volume.

AdonisIQ EditorialOctober 30, 202610–12 min read

Key takeaways: Around 10–15 hard sets per muscle per week is a practical starting point for many trained lifters, not a magic number. Beginners can grow with fewer. Count direct and indirect work sensibly, spread volume across manageable sessions, and add sets only when progress and recovery justify them.

The short answer: start with 10–15 quality sets

For many intermediate lifters, approximately 10–15 challenging sets per muscle per week is a useful starting experiment—not a biological requirement or universal optimum. Beginners can grow on fewer sets, while experienced lifters may benefit from more when recovery and performance support it. The 2026 ACSM position stand found higher weekly volume (at least 10 sets) enhanced hypertrophy across the evidence reviewed, but it could not identify one universally optimal set count.

What actually counts as a hard set?

Count a working set when the target muscle performs meaningful work through a controlled range of motion with substantial effort. A practical default is to finish most working sets with about 1–3 repetitions in reserve (RIR). Warm-ups and very easy technique sets usually do not count toward your growth volume. Training to absolute failure on every set is unnecessary and can add fatigue without reliably adding growth.

Practical weekly set ranges by experience

Beginners: roughly 4–10 challenging sets per muscle per week can be productive. Intermediate lifters: try around 8–15. Advanced lifters: around 10–20 may be useful for selected muscles, depending on training history, exercise choice, effort and recovery. These are coaching ranges, not precise thresholds established by a trial. Start toward the lower end, measure results, and only add work when there is a reason.

Why more sets eventually stop paying off

The 2026 Pelland and colleagues meta-regression pooled 67 studies and found that more weekly volume generally increased hypertrophy, but with diminishing marginal returns. Each extra set is likely to contribute less than earlier sets. The ACSM review similarly discusses a flattening of returns at high weekly volumes. The goal is not to maximize the set counter; it is to maximize productive training you can recover from.

Do compound lifts count for multiple muscles?

Yes, but not always equally. A bench press strongly trains chest and also loads triceps and anterior delts. Rows train back and also involve biceps. Pelland and colleagues found a fractional approach—counting indirect sets as roughly half a set—fit the analyzed data better than counting every involved muscle as a full set. This is an approximation, not a universal formula: exercise technique, range of motion and anatomy change the contribution.

Should you train each muscle once, twice or three times?

When total weekly sets are comparable, training frequency itself appears less important for hypertrophy than total productive volume. Twice weekly is a convenient default because it distributes hard sets across sessions, limits late-session fatigue and creates more chances to practice lifts. A muscle can grow with one exposure, and three exposures may help when the weekly workload is high. Choose the schedule that lets you train consistently.

Example: four-day upper/lower split

Monday upper: chest 5 sets, back 5, side delts 3, direct biceps 2, direct triceps 2. Tuesday lower: quads 5, hamstrings 4, calves 4. Thursday upper: chest 5, back 5, side delts 3, direct biceps 2, direct triceps 2. Friday lower: quads 5, hamstrings 4, calves 4. This yields 10 direct chest and back sets, 10 quad sets, 8 hamstring sets and 8 calf sets; arms also receive indirect work from pressing and pulling. Adjust based on performance rather than treating the numbers as mandatory.

Example: five-day split with a priority muscle

A practical five-day structure is Push, Pull, Legs, Upper, Lower. For a chest priority, distribute 12 direct chest sets as 6 on Push and 6 on Upper. Keep back at 10–12 direct sets across Pull and Upper; quads at 10–12 and hamstrings at 8–10 across the two lower days. Maintain smaller muscles with fewer direct sets if compound work already challenges them. Prioritize the target muscle early in sessions, when technique and effort are freshest.

When should you add or remove sets?

Hold a starting volume steady for 3–5 weeks. Track repetitions, load, technique, soreness, joint comfort and readiness. If lifts progress and recovery is good, you may not need more sets. If progress stalls despite adequate nutrition, sleep and consistent effort, try adding 2 weekly sets to the lagging muscle and reassess. If performance declines, soreness lingers, or joints become irritated, reduce volume or redistribute it before adding anything.

Frequently asked questions

Is 20 sets per muscle too much? Not automatically, but it can be unnecessary or difficult to recover from. Can five sets build muscle? Yes, especially for beginners or when those sets are high quality. Do warm-up sets count? Usually not unless they are sufficiently challenging. Do I need to train to failure? No—sets performed close to failure can be effective without reaching failure every time.
