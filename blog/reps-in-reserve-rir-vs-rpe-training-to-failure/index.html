Reps in Reserve (RIR) Explained:
How Close to Failure Should You Train?

Build muscle without turning every set into an all-out grind. Learn when to push, when to stop, and how to measure effort.

AdonisIQ EditorialNovember 2, 202610–12 min read

Key takeaways: Training near failure can be effective, but absolute failure is not necessary for every set. RIR estimates how many clean reps remain; RPE translates that effort into a familiar 1–10 lifting scale. Start with manageable effort, track progression and adjust to the exercise and recovery.

What does reps in reserve mean?

Reps in reserve (RIR) estimates how many additional repetitions you could complete with acceptable technique before reaching momentary muscular failure. If you finish a set of 10 and believe you could have completed two more clean repetitions, that set was approximately 2 RIR. It is a subjective estimate, not a direct measurement; accuracy improves with practice and may be worse when fatigue, exercise familiarity or motivation changes.

RIR versus RPE: the quick conversion

RIRApproximate RPEEffort
010At the limit
19One clean rep left
28Two reps left
37Three reps left
4–55–6Moderate to challenging

The common resistance-training RPE scale approximately maps 0 RIR to RPE 10, 1 RIR to RPE 9, 2 RIR to RPE 8, 3 RIR to RPE 7, and 4 RIR to RPE 6. The conversion is a coaching shorthand, not an exact law. RPE in endurance training can use a different scale, so always check which scale a workout uses.

Does training to failure build more muscle?

Research suggests that training sufficiently close to failure can stimulate hypertrophy, but taking every set to absolute failure is not required. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis by Grgic and colleagues found no clear overall hypertrophy advantage for momentary muscular failure over non-failure training when studies were pooled. A 2024 meta-regression by Robinson and colleagues suggested that hypertrophy may improve as sets end closer to failure, but the exact optimum remains uncertain. Study definitions and the accuracy of reported RIR vary, so avoid treating one precise number as settled science.

Why pushing every set to failure can backfire

True failure is demanding. It can increase acute fatigue, reduce repetitions on subsequent sets and make recovery harder, particularly on multi-joint lifts that require substantial stabilization. The extra stimulus from the final repetition may not justify its cost every time. More is not always better when your goal is repeatable quality training across the week.

Compound lifts versus isolation exercises

For demanding barbell squats, deadlift variations and heavy presses, keeping around 1–3 RIR on most working sets is a practical starting point; sometimes more is appropriate during skill practice or high-fatigue weeks. For supported machines, curls, lateral raises and leg extensions, working closer to failure may be easier to manage safely. Exercise choice, spotting, technique, injuries and experience matter more than a rigid rule.

How to estimate RIR more accurately

First define what a clean repetition looks like. Track rep speed, technique and consistency. After a set, ask how many additional reps you could complete without changing form or range of motion. Compare predictions with occasional carefully supervised or lower-risk sets taken near failure. Do not test absolute failure on high-risk lifts just to calibrate the number. RIR will remain approximate, and that is fine.

Real-world example: incline press and lateral raises

Suppose an incline press prescription is three sets of 6–10 reps at 2 RIR. If you perform 9 reps and estimate two clean reps remain, record 9 at 2 RIR rather than grinding for an eleventh. On lateral raises, a 12–20-rep target at 1–2 RIR may be practical; stop when the next clean repetition is unlikely, not when momentum could swing the weight higher. These are illustrative examples, not claims about automatic app adjustments.

RIR makes progressive overload more meaningful

Adding one rep is not necessarily progress if last week's set ended at 3 RIR and this week's set ended at 0 RIR. Compare performance at similar effort, technique and range of motion. A set of 10 at 2 RIR becoming 11 at 2 RIR is clearer evidence of progress. When you consistently reach the top of a prescribed rep range at the intended effort, a modest load increase may make sense. Common questions

Is 0 RIR the same as failure? In common lifting usage, 0 RIR means no additional clean reps are available; definitions differ slightly between technical and momentary failure. Is 3 RIR too easy to grow? Not necessarily—sets around 3 RIR can contribute to hypertrophy. Should beginners train to failure? Usually there is little reason to make failure a routine requirement while learning technique. Does RPE 8 always mean 2 RIR? It is an approximation for resistance training, not an exact equivalence.
